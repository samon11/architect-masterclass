## Notes

### Jumping Ahead of the Competition

- Being ahead of the competition starts with being ahead of the people around you — see the **lion tamer effect** under [[Modules/What Comes Next#Propelling Your Business and Career|Propelling Your Business and Career]]: you cannot lead developers you cannot match.

### Propelling Your Business and Career

**The knowledge-ability curve** (from brain studies — every knowledge-intensive ability behaves the same: programming, violin, flying, medicine, even jogging):
- Everyone starts out **sucking** at a new ability — cross the **sucky threshold** and a fork appears
  - One path: "good enough for me" → cruise on the **amateur curve** forever
  - Other path: keep working → cross the **passion threshold** — learning becomes rewarding, thrilling, a dopamine hit → ever-higher expertise
- The chart says **everyone has the same potential** — not that everyone is the same. Experts are common, not genius: millions of doctors, pilots, professional violinists. "The main distinction between the amateur and the professional is not in built-in talent. It's in dedication."

**What separates you:** a built-in disposition of time and focus, a desire to keep improving, using the **micro opportunities** in your daily schedule to concentrate and get better. Nobody forced you to train — it was a deliberate decision.

**The amateur curve / excuse machine:**
- If you're cruising above the sucky threshold, every new thing you learn risks dipping you *below* it again — an unacceptable risk, so amateurs make excuses not to learn
- Classic example: C++ devs abandoned the language at the sight of .NET; VB6 devs called .NET over-engineered, bloated — nobody ever used VB.NET. Different curves, different reactions
- A third category: **dropout developers** — given up, often move into management or marketing

**The lion tamer effect (blood in the water):**
- As an architect, average will not do — you must be beyond the average developer
- Lion tamers aren't safe because lions are fed; they established themselves as the **alpha lion** since cubhood
- If developers sense they can run circles around you, they will never do anything for you — they play **buzzword bingo** with management (Kubernetes, AWS, lambda, ...) and walk away
- You need to be the alpha lion: developers think "she/he will eat me alive" — you see around corners in seven dimensions

**How you actually get better:**
- Not by having a deadline chase you — "you are not an infantile baby, you're an adult and you make the decision to be better"
- Paradox: **if you want to lose your technical proficiency, adopt a deadline / adopt coding responsibility** — you'll have no time for the very things that make you better

## Quiz

> [!question]- 1. Name the two thresholds on the knowledge-ability curve. What happens at each?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> The **sucky threshold** — where you cross from being bad at something to "good enough" (a fork: cruise on the amateur curve or keep going). The **passion threshold** — beyond it, learning new things becomes rewarding and thrilling (dopamine hits), and you climb toward expertise.

> [!question]- 2. What does the brain-study chart actually claim — that everyone is the same, or something else?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> That **everyone has the same potential** — not that everyone is the same. Experts (doctors, pilots, violinists) are common, not geniuses; the difference between amateur and professional is **dedication, not built-in talent**. The chart just doesn't show the people who choose to squander their lives.

> [!question]- 3. Fill in the blank: "The main distinction between the amateur and the professional is not in ____________. It's in ____________."
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> "...not in **built-in talent**. It's in **dedication**."

> [!question]- 4. Why do developers cruising on the amateur curve make every possible excuse not to learn something new? Use the VB6/.NET example.
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> Because learning something new makes you slide back on the curve — and if you're barely above the sucky threshold, you risk dipping **below** it again, which is an unacceptable risk. So VB6 devs called .NET over-engineered and bloated rather than admit the pain of learning it (while C++ devs, above the threshold, embraced it). They practice what they're already good at — and stay average forever.

> [!question]- 5. What are the **micro opportunities** Juval mentions, and why do they matter?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> The small pockets of time in your daily schedule where you can concentrate and get better. Using them deliberately is how you keep improving — it's the built-in disposition of time and focus that separates you from the average developer.

> [!question]- 6. Explain the lion tamer effect. Why is it dangerous for an architect to be (or appear) average?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> Lion tamers aren't safe because lions are fed — they established themselves as the **alpha lion** early, so lions submit. If developers sense they can run circles around you (e.g., playing **buzzword bingo** with management), they'll never really do anything for you. You must be the alpha lion — expert enough that developers believe you'll eat them alive.

> [!question]- 7. What three categories of developer exist on the competence curve?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> 1) **Amateurs cruising** on the amateur curve — stay average forever; 2) **professionals** beyond the passion threshold — keep improving; 3) **dropout developers** — gave up (and you often wish they would), usually moving into management or marketing.

> [!question]- 8. Paradox: what counterintuitive advice does Juval give about getting better? What actually makes you *lose* technical proficiency?
>!
> [!summary]- Answer
> It's exactly the opposite of what you think: **adopting a deadline or adopting coding responsibility** makes you lose technical proficiency, because you'll have no time left for the mercurial aspects that make you better. Improvement comes from the deliberate adult decision to be better — not from being chased by deadlines.
